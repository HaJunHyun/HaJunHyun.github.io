## Overview

A behavior policy offers a useful starting action. But selecting the best action it samples can still leave room for improvement:
better actions may lie nearby, and there may be several different ways to improve the same proposal.

**Proposal-Conditioned Refinement Flows (PReFlow)** learns a distribution of these improvements.
A behavior flow proposes actions, a critic selects a promising proposal, and a second, conditional flow refines it.
This post explains our paper, _Diffusion Policy Improvement with Proposal-Conditioned Refinement Flows_,
by **Junhyun Ha, Juho Lee, and Byungwoo Park**.<d-cite key="ha2026preflow"></d-cite>

The method has two useful properties: the refinement can represent multiple separated modes, and its training targets have a
closed form. The latter removes the need for a backward adjoint solve when training the refinement flow.

## Why refine a proposal

In offline reinforcement learning, a critic $$Q_\psi(s,a)$$ estimates which actions are valuable, while a behavior policy
$$\pi_\beta(a\mid s)$$ models the actions supported by the dataset.
A common policy improvement objective balances value against a KL penalty toward that behavior policy:

$$
\max_\pi\;
\mathbb{E}_{\tilde a\sim\pi(\cdot\mid s)}
\left[Q_\psi(s,\tilde a)\right]
-\frac{1}{\tau}\operatorname{KL}(\pi\Vert\pi_\beta).
$$

At a fixed state, its optimum is

$$
\pi^\star(\tilde a\mid s)
\propto \pi_\beta(\tilde a\mid s)
\exp\!\left(\tau Q_\psi(s,\tilde a)\right).
$$

The behavior-density factor matters: even a high-value action receives little weight if its behavior density is small.
This can discourage useful changes near a likely behavior action.

An alternative is to sample an action first, then improve it locally. But the choice of refinement distribution matters too.
A deterministic editor produces one refinement; a Gaussian editor cannot represent several separated modes for a fixed
state and proposal. A conditional flow gives the refinement distribution more flexibility.

## Select then refine

At a state $$s$$, action generation has three steps:

1. Sample $$K$$ candidate actions from the behavior flow.
2. Select the candidate with the highest critic value.
3. Sample a refinement from a flow conditioned on both the state and the selected proposal.

Writing the selected proposal as $$a$$ and the refinement as $$\Delta a$$, the executed action is

$$
\tilde a = a+\Delta a.
$$

We regularize the refined-action distribution toward $$\mathcal N(a,\delta^2 I)$$.
The scale $$\delta$$ controls the reference neighborhood around the proposal.
For a fixed proposal, the optimal refinement distribution is

$$
\pi^{\mathrm{ref},\star}(\Delta a\mid s,a)
\propto
\exp\!\left(
\tau Q_\psi(s,a+\Delta a)
-\frac{\|\Delta a\|^2}{2\delta^2}
\right).
$$

The first term favors value; the second discourages large changes.
Although the **reference** is Gaussian, the **optimal refinement** need not be:
several peaks in the critic can produce several useful refinement modes.

## A smoother behavior prior

The two stages also have a joint interpretation.
Define the value of a proposal after optimal regularized refinement as

$$
V_\delta(s,a)
=\frac{1}{\tau}\log
\mathbb{E}_{\Delta a\sim\mathcal N(0,\delta^2I)}
\left[\exp\!\left(\tau Q_\psi(s,a+\Delta a)\right)\right].
$$

If both proposal selection and refinement are optimized with the same KL coefficient, the final action distribution is

$$
\pi_\delta^\star(\tilde a\mid s)
\propto
\bar\pi_{\beta,\delta}(\tilde a\mid s)
\exp\!\left(\tau Q_\psi(s,\tilde a)\right),
$$

where

$$
\bar\pi_{\beta,\delta}(\tilde a\mid s)
=\int \pi_\beta(a\mid s)\,
\mathcal N(\tilde a;a,\delta^2I)\,\mathrm da.
$$

This is policy improvement under a **Gaussian-smoothed behavior prior**.
Nearby behavior proposals can supply reference density to an action even when its original behavior density is low.

This result describes the ideal joint optimum. The practical algorithm approximates proposal quality using
$$Q_\psi(s,a)$$ with taylor expansion, and chooses greedily among finitely many samples.

## Training the refinement flow

We parameterize the refinement flow as $$\Delta a=\delta y_1$$, where $$y_1$$ is the endpoint of this conditional flow:

$$
\frac{\mathrm dY_t}{\mathrm dt}
=v_\varphi(Y_t,t\mid s,a),
\qquad Y_0\sim\mathcal N(0,I).
$$

The Gaussian refinement reference makes the training target especially simple.
Given a sampled endpoint $$y_1$$, draw $$t\sim\mathcal U(0,1)$$ and fresh Gaussian noise $$\epsilon$$, then construct

$$
y_t=ty_1+(1-t)\epsilon,
\qquad d(t)=t^2+(1-t)^2.
$$

The adjoint-matching velocity target is

$$
v^{\mathrm{tgt}}
=\frac{2t-1}{d(t)}y_t
+\frac{\tau\delta(1-t)}{d(t)}
\nabla_{\tilde a}Q_\psi(s,a+\delta y_1).
$$

Training the refiner then uses a single velocity regression loss:

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{actor}}(\varphi)
=\mathbb E\left[
\left\|v_\varphi(y_t,t\mid s,a)
-\operatorname{sg}[v^{\mathrm{tgt}}]\right\|_2^2
\right].
$$

Here $$\operatorname{sg}$$ denotes stop-gradient. 
For refinement training, proposals are sampled directly from the behavior policy with $$K=1$$.

Our simulation-free construction only needs one forward flow rollout to generate an endpoint, and optimize efficiently 
via an endpoint critic gradient, no requirement of backward integration of adjoint dynamics.
At inference, the critic scores proposals; the learned refinement flow does not require critic-gradient evaluations.

## Results on OGBench

We evaluate on **50 tasks** across ten OGBench domain variants.
The main protocol uses **1M offline gradient steps**, followed by **500K online environment steps**.
PReFlow and the reproduced baselines use eight seeds per task.

The table below reproduces a subset of the aggregate results in Table 1 of the paper.
Entries are mean success rates, with reported 95% confidence intervals in brackets.

| Method      | Offline          | After online fine-tuning |
| :---------- | ---------------: | -----------------------: |
| QAM-E       | 46% [45, 47]     | 85% [84, 86]             |
| QPILOTS-U   | 44% [42, 47]     | 89% [87, 90]             |
| QPILOTS-M   | 48% [46, 50]     | 90% [87, 91]             |
| Q-Flow      | 48% [47, 48]     | 66% [65, 67]             |
| RQL         | **55% [53, 56]** | 80% [80, 81]             |
| **PReFlow** | 53% [51, 54]     | **91% [90, 92]**         |

The table combines the paper's eight-seed runs of PReFlow, Q-Flow, and RQL with released QPILOTS results
(eight seeds) and QAM-E results (12 seeds), as detailed in Appendix D.3.

PReFlow has the highest aggregate online mean among the methods compared in the paper.
RQL starts with a higher aggregate offline mean, but the ranking changes after online fine-tuning:
PReFlow improves by 38 percentage points, compared with 25 for RQL and 18 for Q-Flow, using the rounded means above.

### Why might PReFlow adapt better online?

Section 5.1 offers a mechanistic hypothesis: **an updated critic can affect both refinement learning and proposal selection directly**.
Q-Flow fits a value function at intermediate flow states using endpoint critic values, then uses its gradients to train the policy.
RQL learns values for individual flow steps in an expanded MDP, using reversed flow trajectories for off-policy learning.
Both methods therefore learn value information within the flow trajectory.<d-cite key="ha2026preflow"></d-cite>

PReFlow constructs its refinement target from the critic gradient at the **final action**,
$$\nabla_{\tilde a}Q_\psi(s,\tilde a)$$.
When online data improves the critic, that new signal can enter the next refinement regression target directly,
without fitting an auxiliary intermediate-state value function.
The same updated critic also changes which behavior proposal wins the selection step.
These two routes provide a plausible explanation for the online gains in Figure 3, even when the offline starting score is modest.

This explanation is a hypothesis supported by the method's structure and observed learning curves;
the comparison does not isolate the causal effect of the value-learning route.
The antmaze-giant configuration also increases the refinement scale from $$\delta=0.1$$ offline to $$0.2$$ online
(Appendix E.5), which should be considered when interpreting that domain's improvement.

## Refinement ablation

To isolate refinement from candidate selection, Table 2 fixes **one proposal**, $$K=1$$.
The base policy executes that proposal directly. The other variants use a learned diagonal Gaussian or a conditional flow
to refine it, with the same conditional objective and refinement scale $$\delta=0.1$$.

The table reproduces offline success rates (%) after 1M gradient steps, including the paper's reported $$\pm$$ values.
Each domain covers five tasks, with three seeds per task; Appendix D.5 gives the controlled settings.

| Domain                | Base       | Gaussian    | Flow           |
| :-------------------- | ---------: | ----------: | -------------: |
| antmaze-large         | 2.3 ± 0.8  | 76.3 ± 7.2  | **79.0 ± 7.5** |
| humanoidmaze-medium   | 2.7 ± 1.0  | 57.1 ± 8.5  | **69.2 ± 1.2** |
| scene                 | 3.9 ± 1.5  | 68.1 ± 3.2  | **73.1 ± 0.8** |
| cube-double           | 2.0 ± 0.0  | 40.8 ± 5.2  | **53.9 ± 3.3** |

Both refiners substantially improve the behavior proposal. Flow refinement has the higher mean in all four domains,
including gains over Gaussian refinement of 12.1 points on humanoidmaze-medium and 13.1 points on cube-double.
Because $$K=1$$ throughout, these gains support the benefit of the refinement policy class independently of choosing among more proposals.

## Selection ablation

We next keep the learned refiner and vary the number of behavior proposals, $$K\in\{1,2,4,8\}$$.
With $$K=1$$, the single proposal is refined directly. For $$K>1$$, the critic selects the highest-value proposal before refinement.
Figure 5 shows that selection can improve both offline learning and final online performance, with benefits already visible at $$K=2$$.

To separate the effect of **critic-guided choice** from simply drawing more proposals, Table 6 compares selection rules at the same $$K$$.
Uniform selection picks a candidate at random, softmax samples in proportion to $$\exp(\tau Q_\psi(s,a_i))$$,
and argmax chooses the candidate with the highest critic value. All three then apply the learned flow refiner.

The table below rearranges the $$K=4$$ and $$K=8$$ results from Table 6.
Entries are offline success rates (%) after 1M gradient steps: mean ± standard deviation over three seeds,
with each seed averaged over five tasks. Softmax uses $$\tau=30$$ on humanoidmaze-medium and $$\tau=3$$ on scene and cube-double.
The $$K=1$$ flow results are listed in the refinement table above.

| Domain              | K   | Uniform    | Softmax        | Argmax         |
| :------------------ | --: | ---------: | -------------: | -------------: |
| humanoidmaze-medium | 4   | 68.8 ± 2.6 | **70.1 ± 3.1** | 70.0 ± 2.9     |
| humanoidmaze-medium | 8   | 68.7 ± 0.8 | 66.0 ± 3.3     | **69.2 ± 3.4** |
| scene               | 4   | 74.0 ± 1.7 | 89.5 ± 0.8     | **94.9 ± 1.0** |
| scene               | 8   | 74.1 ± 0.8 | 90.7 ± 1.7     | **92.8 ± 0.6** |
| cube-double         | 4   | 50.3 ± 6.9 | 61.3 ± 1.0     | **63.3 ± 4.2** |
| cube-double         | 8   | 49.6 ± 8.0 | **60.1 ± 3.9** | 58.7 ± 4.1     |

At $$K=4$$, argmax improves over uniform selection by 20.9 points on scene and 13.0 points on cube-double;
the offline difference on humanoidmaze-medium is small.
Argmax has the highest mean in four of these six settings, supporting the choice used in the benchmark.
The gains depend on the domain and candidate count: increasing $$K$$ from 4 to 8 does not consistently improve performance.
